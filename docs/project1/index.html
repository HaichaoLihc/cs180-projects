01

The problem & the matching metric

Three exposures. One color image.

The input is a tall grayscale scan: the blue-filter exposure at the top, green in the middle, and red at the bottom. Simply stacking these as RGB produces colored double edges because the camera and subjects moved between exposures. The task is to find the translation of G and R relative to B.

The scan is split into three equal-height arrays, discarding at most two trailing rows. Integer values are converted to floats in [0, 1] using the original dtype range: 255 for 8-bit JPEGs and 65,535 for the supplied 16-bit TIFFs. The additional LoC TIFFs are 8-bit. Alignment is estimated at the input resolution; only website previews are resized.

Compare structure through intensity

For each candidate shift, normalized cross-correlation (NCC) measures how closely the two interior intensity patterns agree after subtracting their means and dividing by their lengths. The best shift maximizes NCC.

NCC(A, B) = Σ (A − Ā)(B − B̄)
/ √[Σ(A − Ā)² · Σ(B − B̄)²]

Mean subtraction removes an overall brightness offset, and normalization handles a uniform positive contrast change. NCC does not remove changes in how different colored objects respond to each filter.

Keep the comparison fair

A 10% border on each side is excluded from scoring. The margin is enlarged if needed so every displacement uses the same reference rectangle and all samples stay inside the moving image. No wrapped pixels enter the metric.

The code also implements L2 by minimizing mean squared intensity error. This has the same optimum as the L2 norm for a fixed sample count. All primary results below use NCC; L2 is an alternative executable option, not a second reported experiment.

Offset convention: every pair is (x, y), the translation applied to G or R to align it with B. Positive x moves right; positive y moves down. For example, Cathedral’s green channel moves 2 pixels right and 5 down.

02

Single scale · raw-intensity NCC

Start small: exhaustive alignment.

Two nested loops test every integer displacement in [−15, 15] × [−15, 15]: 961 candidates per moving channel. The three JPEG experiments score every interior pixel. The highest-scoring G and R shifts are applied with array rolls, then the arrays are stacked in R, G, B order.

The saved image keeps only the common valid area of the three shifted channels, removing pixels that wrapped around. Historical black, white, and colored scan borders can still remain; overlap trimming is not content-aware border detection.

All three low-resolution images align consistently. Their offsets also match the independent pyramid run below. Remaining fringes at scan boundaries are outside the interior used for matching.

03

Multi-scale · raw-intensity NCC

Search coarsely. Refine precisely.

The full TIFFs contain roughly 3,200 × 3,700 pixels per channel, with offsets much larger than 15 pixels. A wide exhaustive search at this resolution would score thousands of large arrays. An image pyramid makes a large displacement small enough to search economically.

01 / ReduceBuild the pyramid

Smooth with a Gaussian (σ = 1), then keep every second row and column. Repeat until the longest side is at most 300 pixels.

02 / LocateSearch the smallest level

Evaluate the full ±15-pixel window with NCC. A shift of one pixel here represents up to 16 pixels in the original TIFF.

03 / RefineDouble and correct

At each finer level, multiply the current offset by two and search ±3 around that estimate: just 49 candidates.

04 / ReconstructReturn to full resolution

Refine integer shifts on the original-size arrays, align G and R to B, and retain the common valid rectangle.

The pyramid is built explicitly using Gaussian smoothing and decimation, with no high-level pyramid or image-registration function. Both NCC and edge experiments use one fixed parameter set across every input; no offsets are manually selected.

For speed, each pyramid-level score samples a regular grid of at most about 60,000 interior pixels. The stride is ⌈√(interior area / 60,000)⌉, with a minimum of 1. Candidate shifts are still tested in single-pixel increments, including at the finest level. This is an approximation to dense NCC; sampling can miss fine texture. The code exposes a dense-scoring option with --max-samples 0.

05

Bells & whistles · better features

When brightness is misleading.

The Emir’s blue robe is bright through the blue filter but much darker through the red filter. Raw-intensity NCC can prefer an incorrect translation because the relationship is object-specific, rather than one uniform brightness change. Its red-channel estimate is far from the correct alignment.

For the extension, each intensity-pyramid level is smoothed with σ = 1. Sobel horizontal and vertical derivatives are combined as gradient magnitude, E = √(Iₓ² + Iᵧ²). The same NCC search now compares these edge-strength images. Architectural lines, facial contours, and fabric boundaries remain useful even when their colors change brightness.

Same crop, original scan coordinates

Detail crops use the same rectangle before overlap trimming: x = 1,000…2,599 and y = 500…2,099. They are resized only for display. The differing full-image frames above come from each alignment’s valid overlap.

Edge-based offsets for every scan

The extension is run on all 17 inputs with the same parameters. The raw NCC results remain the baseline; the edge outputs provide a second, reproducible reconstruction.

Edge alignment removes the Emir’s large translation failure and makes small corrections on other scans. It does not recover missing detail, remove scratches, undo motion between exposures, or model rotation and scale. Repeated edges and textureless regions can still make the score ambiguous.

06

Bells & whistles · automatic contrast

A restrained contrast adjustment.

After edge alignment, a shared linear intensity stretch uses the pooled RGB 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles as black and white points. The percentiles are estimated on every fourth row and column, then applied to every pixel.

I′ = clip((I − p₀.₅) / (p₉₉.₅ − p₀.₅), 0, 1)

For Church, the measured limits are 0.0499 and 1.0000. The same mapping is applied to all three channels, avoiding independent color-channel stretching. Percentiles make the adjustment less sensitive to a few extreme pixels than using the absolute minimum and maximum.

This is a modest display enhancement, not color calibration or white balance. It can clip the darkest and brightest tails and can be influenced by scan borders. The main result galleries retain the original intensities so the alignment experiments can be judged separately.

07

Discussion & reproducibility

What holds up, and what remains.

The useful simplification

Integer translation and a common interior explain most of the large color fringes. The pyramid makes large offsets tractable, while NCC tolerates uniform changes in brightness and contrast. Edges help where the relationship between channels depends on the material or color of an object.

The raw NCC baseline has one clear global failure among these 17 reconstructions: Emir. The edge version corrects it. These judgments are based on visual inspection; there are no ground-truth registration labels for these historic scans.

The limits of the model

A single translation cannot align a person who moved independently, changing water, or every point in an image with a slight rotation or scale difference. Local fringes and historical border artifacts are therefore not all evidence of a wrong global shift.

No content-aware border detector, white-balance estimator, geometric warp, or calibrated color mapping is included. Removing only the common-overlap boundaries is deliberate, and the opening image’s additional 6% crop is a presentation choice.

Reproducibility. The separate code submission contains the alignment implementation, dataset source manifest and downloader, batch runner, measured offset files, dependency versions, and README. Five synthetic tests verify offset signs, brightness invariance, large shifts within the pyramid’s capture range, odd-sized levels, L2/edge modes, 16-bit normalization, and removal of wrapped pixels.

Python 3.10.11 · NumPy 2.2.6 · SciPy 1.15.3 · Pillow 11.3.0. Timing was measured on the local macOS machine. Pyramids have a finite capture range: shifts outside the coarse search window can converge to an incorrect result.
